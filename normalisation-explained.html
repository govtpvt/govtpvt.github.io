Normalisation in Government Exams, Explained With an Example

Two candidates score the same raw marks in the same exam and end up with different final scores. Nothing has gone wrong. This is normalisation, and it applies to almost every large exam held in more than one shift.

Why it exists

When several lakh candidates sit an exam, it cannot be held in one sitting. Each shift gets a different question paper, and no two papers are equally hard. Without an adjustment, candidates in an easier shift would have an unfair advantage. Normalisation puts all shifts on one scale before a merit list is made.

The idea in one example

Shift A (easier paper)Shift B (harder paper)
Average raw score120 out of 200100 out of 200
Your raw score130130
Where you stand in your shiftA little above averageWell above average

A raw 130 is a stronger performance in Shift B than in Shift A, because the Shift B paper was harder. After normalisation the Shift B candidate receives the higher final score. The figures here are only an illustration.

The two common approaches

  • Percentile-based. Your score is converted to the percentage of candidates in your own shift who scored the same as you or lower. Percentiles from all shifts are then merged into one list. Entrance tests commonly use this.
  • Formula-based. Raw marks are adjusted using the averages and spread of marks in each shift, so that the shifts line up. Several recruitment exams use a formula of this kind.

The exact method is always printed in the notification or in a separate notice from the conducting body. Read that, because the details differ.

What it means for you

  • Your normalised score can be higher or lower than your raw score.
  • You cannot choose or predict your shift, so there is no shift strategy.
  • Cut-offs are applied to normalised scores, not raw marks.
  • A score you calculate from the answer key is a raw score. Treat it as an estimate.
Practical step: when the answer key for your exam is released, work out your raw score, then wait for the official scorecard before comparing yourself with the cut-off published alongside the result and merit list.

Common misunderstandings

  • It is not grace marking. Marks are rescaled, not gifted.
  • It does not favour any category. The same method applies to everyone in the exam.
  • It cannot be challenged question by question. Objections are raised against the answer key, within its window, not against the normalisation method.

Whether a recruitment uses normalisation is stated in the notification; you can check it before you apply from the latest government job notifications. Your shift and reporting time arrive with the admit card, and multi-shift entrance tests are listed with admission notices.
